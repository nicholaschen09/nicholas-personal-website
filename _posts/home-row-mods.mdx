I spend at least eight hours a day on a computer. That means my hands live in the same awkward staggered-keyboard posture for most of my waking life.

At some point my wrists started complaining louder than my brain. I wanted to type faster, but I also wanted to stop doing the wrist yoga that normal keyboards force you into. So I started looking into layouts, split boards, layers, and eventually home row mods.

One thing that becomes obvious once you pay attention: your hands are not evenly distributed over a full-size keyboard. Your left hand covers way fewer keys than your right. That imbalance is where a lot of the awkward reaching comes from.

## Home row mods

The home row is where your fingers are supposed to rest. The bumps on `f` and `j` are there so your index fingers can find home without looking.

Home row mods take that idea further. Instead of reaching for `ctrl`, `shift`, `alt`, or `cmd`, those modifiers live under your home row keys. Tap the key and you get the letter. Hold the key and it becomes a modifier.

For example:

- tap `a` → `a`
- hold `a` → modifier
- tap `s` → `s`
- hold `s` → another modifier

The point is not to make the keyboard more complicated. The point is to stop leaving the home row every three seconds.

## The setup

I did this with [Kanata](https://github.com/jtroo/kanata), a keyboard remapper that lets you define tap-hold behavior and layers without flashing custom firmware.

My keyboards are very normal:

- Legion 5 Pro laptop keyboard
- Redragon Kumara K552

That was intentional. I wanted to see how far I could get before buying or building a split board.

## The layout

The exact bindings matter less than the rules behind them.

Most common modifiers should be the easiest to hold. Keys I fat-finger the most should not sit in dangerous places. Timing is what makes or breaks the whole thing: too short and normal typing turns into accidental shortcuts, too long and every shortcut feels laggy.

So the layout is less a keymap and more a negotiation between comfort, speed, and how much nonsense muscle memory is willing to relearn.

## The learning curve

Setting up Kanata was the easy part. Retraining years of muscle memory was not.

It took about a week before it stopped feeling cursed. Even now, backspace is still the weird one. That key has been carrying a lot of baggage.

## Layers vs home row mods

I got the layers idea from split keyboards.

On a split board, you often hold a thumb key and the same physical keys temporarily become arrows, numbers, symbols, navigation, media controls — whatever you mapped. Home row mods are mostly about bringing modifiers closer. Layers are about making the whole keyboard smaller and more programmable.

The tradeoff looks something like this:

- home row mods are easier to add to a normal keyboard
- layers are more powerful once you commit
- home row mods can interfere with fast letter rolls
- layers can feel like learning a second keyboard
- both are solving the same problem: stop reaching so much

I do not think one is strictly better. Home row mods are the easier on-ramp. Layers are what you eventually build the board around.

If you are still on a full-size keyboard, home row mods are easier to adopt. Layers make the keyboard programmatically smaller, but the physical keys are still sitting there tempting you. I mapped backspace to `[` and disabled the physical backspace key, and I still reached for the empty spot because the key was there. On a 34-key board, that temptation disappears.

I have not given up on layers. If you only use ~34 keys, you get a lot more room for everything else — which is a good segue to what comes next.

## Was it worth it?

Probably.

If you just want to type a little faster, this might be overkill. I am already 130+ WPM on full-size QWERTY. For me the appeal is comfort, fewer reaches, and making the keyboard feel like something I control instead of something I tolerate.

## What's next

The obvious next step is a split keyboard — maybe buy one, maybe build one from scratch. Either way, home row mods made one thing clear: the default layout is just one very popular opinion. The keyboard is a programmable interface. Do not make it boring.
